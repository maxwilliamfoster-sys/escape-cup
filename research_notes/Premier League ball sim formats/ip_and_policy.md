# IP and platform-policy risks: Premier League club physics-simulation videos on TikTok

Research notes compiled 24-26 November 2026. This is research, not legal advice. UK-based creator, age 16. Covers UK law (Trade Marks Act 1994, copyright) and TikTok's global policy. A usage-limit pause interrupted the research, which hit roughly 16 tool calls. Some official pages (the UK IPO register, the Creator Rewards terms) could not be read, and those points are listed under Gaps.

## Q1. Are Premier League club crests and names registered trademarks, and who owns them?

### Takeaway
Club crests and club names belong to the individual clubs, which protect them actively. The Premier League owns its own name, emblem (the lion) and the trophy, and it also licenses club crests collectively for some products. Crests carry two separate rights: they are trade marks, and they are also copyright artistic works. That second layer is the one that matters most for videos.

### Cited Findings
- Tottenham Hotspur says it owns "a number of trademarks including words & logos", which together with design rights and copyright works make up its intellectual property. It also says it "takes the protection of the Club's intellectual property rights very seriously". The page does not say which marks are registered and does not mention fan use or social media. — [Tottenham Hotspur, Brand Protection](https://tottenhamhotspur.com/the-club/brand-protection)
- Football Association Premier League Ltd and others v Panini UK Ltd [2003] EWCA Civ 995: the Court of Appeal unanimously held that Panini's unofficial stickers infringed **copyright** in the club badges and the Premier League emblem. Panini's "incidental inclusion" defence failed. The court reasoned that the badges were what made the images look authentic, and that authenticity was what collectors wanted. — [National Archives judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2003/995); [Swan Turton commentary](https://swanturton.com/incidental-inclusion-defence-fails-for-club-badges-in-unofficial-football-stickers/); [IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2003/07/incidental-inclusion-copyright.html)
- In that case the Premier League, acting for the clubs, had granted Topps exclusive rights "to use and reproduce official team crests and logos" for stickers. This shows the League can license and enforce crests collectively on the clubs' behalf. — [Panini case commentaries, as summarised in search results](https://www.mondaq.com/uk/copyright/22479/the-football-association-premier-league-ltd-and-others-v-panini-uk-ltd)
- Club names act as trade marks ("badges of origin"), but not every name can be registered. Liverpool FC's application to register the word LIVERPOOL for a range of goods was refused as a geographical name under s.3(1)(c) TMA 1994. The club's stated aim was to stop unofficial merchandise. — [Law Society of Scotland Journal, "Home defeat for Liverpool"](https://www.lawscot.org.uk/members/journal/issues/vol-65-issue-03/home-defeat-for-liverpool); [CITMA, "Own goal for Liverpool?"](https://www.citma.org.uk/resources/own-goal-for-liverpool-blog.html); [Bates Wells TM update](https://bateswells.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/BW_TMUpdate_LiverpoolFC_Spr2020.pdf)
- Arsenal Football Club plc v Reed is the leading UK and EU case on club trade marks used on unofficial merchandise sold outside the ground. — [vLex case page](https://vlex.co.uk/vid/arsenal-football-club-plc-793402545); [BAILII 2001 High Court judgment](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Ch/2001/440.html)
- The Premier League trophy: in January 2022 a legal intervention by the Premier League led John Terry to remove the Premier League trophy from NFT images he was promoting. — [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/sport/soccer/english-soccer/legal-intervention-forces-john-terry-to-remove-premier-league-trophy-from-nfts-1.4788043); [Yahoo Sport UK](https://uk.sports.yahoo.com/news/john-terry-removes-premier-league-152324147.html)
- An INTA toolkit for football clubs advises that clubs should own the IP in their crests and names. — [INTA WFS toolkit (PDF)](https://www.inta.org/wp-content/uploads/public-files/perspectives/industry-research/WFS_TOOLKIT_120522.pdf)

### Inferences
- Crests are the highest-risk element. A redrawn or "lightly altered" crest can still be treated as a copy of the artistic work. Copyright claims are easier for rights holders to file and quicker for platforms to act on than trade mark claims, which turn on context.
- The trophy and the lion emblem are actively policed by the Premier League itself, as the Terry case and the emblem's inclusion in Panini show. Don't use them.
- Club names in plain text cannot be protected by copyright (single words and short names are not copyright works). The only possible claim is trade mark, and referential use is a defence to that (see Q4).
- Kit colours on their own (red, sky blue, claret and blue) are generally not protectable as marks. Some clubs have registered colour or shape marks in narrow forms, but I found no registration for plain club colour combinations (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve UK IPO register entries, so I cannot list registration numbers, classes (for example class 41 entertainment or class 9 digital media) or owners for specific crests, club names, "PREMIER LEAGUE" or the lion emblem. A search of the [UK IPO trade mark register](https://trademarks.ipo.gov.uk/ipo-tmtext) would confirm this.
- I found no current (2024-2026) Premier League brand-guidelines or legal-notice page that addresses fan or creator use.
- I did not check whether the current Premier League font (a custom typeface) is registered or licensed. Treat it as proprietary and avoid it; this is an inference.

## Q2. Do the Premier League or clubs take down fan or simulation accounts that use crests or names?

### Takeaway
There is documented enforcement against fan accounts using **crests**, though the best-documented case is old (Manchester United on Twitter, 2014). I found **no documented case** of the League or a club taking down a marble-race or ball-simulation account, or any account for using club names in text. The League's large-scale social media enforcement targets pirated match footage, not graphics.

### Cited Findings
- March 2014: Manchester United's brand protection department had a fan's account suspended. The @ManUtd_Players account had about 100 followers and listed every Man Utd player, and its profile picture was the club crest. The complaint reportedly cited a "breach of copyright laws". The fan said his bio made clear the account was unofficial. The account was later restored with a different avatar. — [Sports Mole, 7 March 2014](https://www.sportsmole.co.uk/off-the-pitch/man-utd/news/man-utd-fan-ordered-to-remove-twitter-badge_142916.html); [World IP Review, 4 March 2014](https://www.worldipreview.com/news/manchester-united-fan-targeted-over-100-follower-twitter-account-6315)
- The Premier League reported detecting more than 645,000 infringing live streams and nearly 900,000 unauthorised recorded match clips in one season. This enforcement is aimed at match footage. — [Premier League and Astro release, Oct 2025](https://corporate.astro.com.my/mediaroom-releases/premier-league-and-astro-warn-malaysian-football-fans-on-the-real-world-risks-of-piracy); [Advanced Television, 1 Oct 2025](https://www.advanced-television.com/2025/10/01/premier-league-astro-warn-of-piracy-risks/)
- In December 2024 FACT (the anti-piracy body) was reported to have sent takedown requests to X, Facebook and TikTok, in the context of illegal streaming. — [SPORTbible, Dec 2024](https://www.sportbible.com/football/premier-league/amazon-fire-sticks-iptv-premier-league-170132-20241206)
- Clubs also pursue unofficial merchandise that uses altered badges. Liverpool FC acted over unofficial shirts. — [Jewish News](https://www.jewishnews.co.uk/liverpool-fc-takes-action-over-fake-palestine-football-shirts/)
- Manchester United has also targeted counterfeit sellers on Twitter. — [ESPN](https://africa.espn.com/football/story/_/id/37337088/manchester-united-target-twitter-counterfeits)
- Roblox developer forum users discussing football crests in games argued that changing only a crest's outline doesn't avoid infringement, and recommended original club identities. This is forum opinion, not law. — [Roblox DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/can-this-logo-get-a-copyright-strike-help/407727)

### Inferences
- The realistic threat is a crest-based copyright or trade mark report from a club's brand protection team. This is more likely once a video goes viral or the account starts making money. Small accounts are not immune: the 2014 account had about 100 followers.
- Enforcement is selective and complaint-driven. Many crest-using fan and sim accounts are probably never reported, but that is luck, not permission.

### Gaps
- I found no reported takedown of a marble-race, ball-simulation or edit account for club crests or names in 2023-2026, on TikTok or YouTube. This may mean such takedowns are rare, or that they happen quietly and are never reported. I can't tell which.
- I found no TikTok transparency data broken down by sports rights holder.

## Q3. How does TikTok handle trade mark reports, and what happens to the account?

### Takeaway
TikTok's own IP policy says that accurate reference, commentary, comparison and fan pages are **generally not trade mark infringement**, as long as you don't claim to be or speak for the brand. Reported content can still be removed, and repeat infringers can be banned. Copyright and trade mark strikes are counted separately, so a crest can trigger a copyright strike even where the name alone would be fine.

### Cited Findings
- TikTok defines trade mark infringement as unauthorised use of an identical or similar mark in a way likely to cause confusion about source, sponsorship or affiliation. — [TikTok Intellectual Property Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/copyright-policy/en)
- The same policy says that using another's trade mark to accurately reference, lawfully comment on, criticise, parody or review is generally not a violation. Fan pages are generally allowed without permission if the user doesn't claim to speak for or be affiliated with the brand. — [TikTok Intellectual Property Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/copyright-policy/en)
- How a report is handled: the rights holder files a Trademark Infringement Report online or in-app. TikTok may share the reporter's details with the user, infringing content may be taken down, and the user gets an in-app notification. The user can appeal in-app with evidence, or ask the owner to retract the report. Removed content is deleted after a period and can't be restored after that. — [TikTok Intellectual Property Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/copyright-policy/en)
- Account consequences: TikTok has a repeat-infringer policy under which it may ban accounts that repeatedly infringe trade marks. It may ban immediately for severe violations and may refuse new accounts to banned users. — [TikTok Intellectual Property Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/copyright-policy/en)
- Strike thresholds: a mirror of TikTok's help page states a limit of 3 strikes for each IP type before permanent removal, with copyright and trade mark strikes counted separately. A third-party guide says TikTok doesn't publish an exact number. — [Mirror of TikTok "Trademark and counterfeiting" help page](https://gamestartup.netlify.app/host-https-support.tiktok.com/en/safety-hc/account-and-user-safety/trademark-and-counterfeiting); conflicting: [Red Points guide](https://www.redpoints.com/blog/how-to-get-someones-tiktok-video-taken-down/). The official help URL now redirects to [tiktok.com/support/faq_detail?id=7543604787682581048](https://www.tiktok.com/support/faq_detail?id=7543604787682581048), which would not render for the fetch tool.
- TikTok says strikes generally expire after 90 days for the purpose of a permanent ban. — [TikTok, Account bans and restrictions](https://support.tiktok.com/en/safety-hc/account-and-user-safety/account-bans-and-restrictions)
- Copyright has a separate DMCA-style notice and counter-notice process, plus fair dealing and EU quotation, criticism and parody exceptions. — [TikTok Intellectual Property Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/copyright-policy/en)

### Inferences
- Under TikTok's own rules, "Arsenal vs Chelsea" as text on coloured balls is the referential or fan use it describes as generally permitted, provided nothing suggests the account is official.
- An account built on crests is exposed to two strike counts (copyright and trade mark). A single club reporting several videos at once could plausibly reach the threshold quickly. That last point is an inference, not a documented case.

### Gaps
- Whether the 3-strikes-per-IP-type rule is still current as of November 2026: the mirror page is undated, and the official page could not be read.

## Q4. Is descriptive or nominative use of club names tolerated, and is an invented league name safer?

### Takeaway
Using a club's name in plain text to say who the ball represents is the safest form of reference. UK law (s.11(2) TMA 1994 referential use, subject to "honest practices") supports it, and so does TikTok's policy. An invented league name removes the most aggressively policed brand entirely. The things that undermine the defence are anything implying official status or sponsorship: crests, the official font, the lion, the trophy, or "official" wording.

### Cited Findings
- Section 11(2) TMA 1994 says a registered trade mark is not infringed by (a) an individual's own name or address, (b) signs which are not distinctive or which concern kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin or other characteristics, or (c) use "for the purpose of identifying or referring to goods or services as those of the proprietor of that trade mark". All three are subject to the proviso that use is "in accordance with honest practices in industrial or commercial matters". — [legislation.gov.uk, s.11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/11)
- Commentary on *British Sugar v James Robertson* (the "Treat" case) notes that s.11(2) won't protect a word that is descriptive in isolation if, in context, it is being used as a trade mark for the defendant's own goods. Context decides. — [Mondaq, "The European Treat"](https://www.mondaq.com/uk/family/4948/the-european-treat-s112b-trade-marks-act-1994)
- TikTok's policy (see Q3) treats accurate reference and fan pages that don't claim affiliation as generally permissible. — [TikTok Intellectual Property Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/copyright-policy/en)
- Panini shows that the authenticity added by official badges is exactly what courts treat as non-incidental, infringing use. — [Swan Turton](https://swanturton.com/incidental-inclusion-defence-fails-for-club-badges-in-unofficial-football-stickers/)

### Inferences
Each item below is graded as an inference from the sources above, not a ruling.
- **Lower risk:** full or short club names in a plain, generic font ("Arsenal", "Man City", "Spurs") as labels; plain kit colours on a ball; generic shapes; your own channel name and branding; an invented competition name such as "Escape Cup"; an "unofficial / not affiliated with any club or league" line in the bio.
- **Medium risk:** nicknames that are themselves registered marks (for example "The Gunners" or "Spurs" may be registered; not checked); club names in a channel or handle name, which looks more like trading on the club's brand than referring to it; selling anything that uses club names.
- **High risk:** official crests (even redrawn ones), the Premier League name used as the title of your competition, the lion emblem, the trophy, the official Premier League font, match footage or broadcast graphics, and official anthems or music.
- **Player names:** not investigated in depth. Real names of famous players are often registered marks, and using them could raise image-rights or false-endorsement issues. Using them as labels is referential, but it adds risk without much benefit for a ball sim. Avoid unless needed.
- Saying "Premier League clubs" descriptively in a caption (for example "every Premier League club races") is probably referential. Calling your own competition "the Premier League" is not.

### Gaps
- I found no UK case directly applying s.11(2)(c) to club names in online entertainment or video content.
- I did not check whether specific short names or nicknames are separately registered.

## Q5. Does monetisation (TikTok Creator Rewards) change the risk?

### Takeaway
Yes, in two ways. Legally, s.11 asks about "honest practices in industrial or commercial matters", so commercial use draws more scrutiny and gives rights holders more reason to act. Practically, the age limit means a 16-year-old can't join Creator Rewards yet anyway.

### Cited Findings
- Creator Rewards requirements reported by third parties: 18 or older, at least 10,000 followers, at least 100,000 views in the last 30 days, videos longer than one minute, and original content. Duets, Stitches and sponsored posts don't count. — [RouteNote](https://routenote.com/blog/tiktok-launch-creator-rewards-program/); [ManyChat](https://manychat.com/blog/tiktok-creator-rewards-program/); [Toptal](https://www.toptal.com/creator/post/how-to-join-the-tiktok-creator-rewards-program); [TikTok Newsroom launch post, 18 Mar 2024](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/introducing-the-new-creator-rewards-program)
- TikTok's brand-partnership terms (TikTok One) require creators to represent that their content is their own original work, except for licensed material. These are a different programme from Creator Rewards. — [TikTok One Creator Program Terms](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-one-creator-program-terms)
- The s.11 defences carry the "honest practices in industrial or commercial matters" proviso. — [legislation.gov.uk, s.11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/11)

### Inferences
- At 16, the creator can't join Creator Rewards (age 18 or over), so monetisation risk is limited to brand deals or merchandise for now. Selling anything with club names or crests (shirts, prints) is the highest-risk activity, as Arsenal v Reed and Liverpool's action on shirts show.
- Using club IP in content that earns money gives clubs a stronger reason and more grounds to complain. It also creates a risk that videos are flagged as "unoriginal" or ineligible for rewards. That last point is unconfirmed.

### Gaps
- I could not read the official Creator Rewards eligibility and terms pages. The help page rendered as navigation only, so its exact current requirements and IP clauses are unverified.
- I found nothing on whether TikTok demonetises videos that use third-party logos.

## Q6. What do popular club-themed simulation accounts actually do (crests vs colours)?

### Takeaway
I could not find reliable evidence of a specific viral club-simulation account's practices or its enforcement history. The nearest documented example, the Marble Soccer app and the "Bouncy Marble" channel, uses country colours and user-made custom clubs rather than official crests.

### Cited Findings
- Marble Soccer is a free iOS football marble simulator. Its listing advertises "100 colors and 217 countries" and lets users create custom tournaments and clubs. The developer's YouTube channel is "Bouncy Marble". — [App Store, Marble Soccer](https://apps.apple.com/app/marble-soccer/id1618696491)

### Inferences
- Using country flags or colours plus custom clubs is a common low-risk design pattern. National flags are generally not owned the way club crests are, though national federation badges are.
